[02:07] In short, the Expectation-Maximization (EM) algorithm is a quite powerful iterative optimization method that’s used to esimate parameters in statistical models when there is missing or incomplete data such as the parameters of our gaussians in the GMM model, and it consists of iteratively applying two steps: The expectation step: where the algorithm calculates the expected value of the log-likelihood function given the current parameter estimates. And th maximization step where the algorithm updates the parameter estimates to maximize
[02:41] the expected log-likelihood calculated in the E-step. So, in other words, what happens is that we are bootstrapping from our initial guess by alternating between the E-step and the M-step, allowing us to refine our parameter estimates. For the Gaussian Mixture Models this translates to firstly having an initial guess of where each gaussian should be centred, how their covariance matrix should look like and how much weight we should provide to each gaussian in the final distribution.
[03:13] All these are the latent or hidden variables of our model that we don’t know and that we are trying to estimate. And now, let’s look at the mathematics of gaussian mixture models. Simply put, the probability of a sample x is equal to the weighted sum of the probability that the sample has in each gaussian. When fitting this model, in the expectation step, we compute gamma hat ik which is the probability that the ith sample was generated by the kth gaussian by dividing the weighted
[03:46] probability of that sample to be produced by the kth gaussian with the weighted sum over the probabilities that the ith sample was generated by each jth gaussian. Then in the maximization step, we recompute our parameter estimates of the gaussian mixture model. Firstly, the new weight of each kth gaussian is computed by averaging the probabilities of all points belonging to that cluster. Secondly, the mean of each gaussian u hat k is calculated as the weighted sum by gamma
[04:20] ik of all points. Finally, we calculate the new variance in the same way as the weighted variance by gamma ik between each data point and the newly estimated u hat k. Then, we alternate between the E step and the M step until we don’t observe significant improvements in our parameter estimates. That’s when the GMM model would have converged and we can use the learned parameters to either draw new samples or make inferences using the Bayes rule. And that’s basically how the GMM model works!
